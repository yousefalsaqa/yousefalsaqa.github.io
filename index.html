System 01 / Teardown

Inside the CAST simulator

A donated CPR manikin, a custom RFID stethoscope, and a Raspberry Pi. In use at Queen's School of Nursing.

  1. RFID reader

    An MFRC522 board in the stethoscope head reads the tag it is resting on, over SPI to the Pi.

  2. Tag location

    12 passive tags sit at anatomical positions inside the manikin: 6 cardiac (H1 to H6), 6 pulmonary (L1 to L6).

  3. Audio trigger

    The matching heart or lung sound reaches the earpieces in under 260 ms, fast enough to feel immediate.

  4. Scenario state

    A Raspberry Pi 4 in the enclosure holds the active scenario, one of 28 clinical audio scenarios.

  5. Instructor control

    A Flask and Socket.IO web interface lets the instructor switch scenarios mid-session, without touching the manikin.

  6. Hardware cost

    About $250 in total hardware. Commercial simulators start around $50K.

01 Physical interaction

A nursing student places the stethoscope on one of 12 auscultation points on the manikin.

02 RFID detection

The reader identifies which tag sits under the chest surface.

03 Scenario processing

The Pi maps tag position and active scenario to the correct clinical sound.

04 Audio response

Playback switches in under 260 ms.

05 Instructor workflow

The instructor drives the session from the web interface while the student examines the manikin.

The challenge was not making one component work. It was making the entire experience feel invisible to the learner.

The photo above is the working prototype exactly as it sat on the shop bench: enclosure, stethoscope head, standard earbuds. The manikin it plugs into was donated by Queen's Clinical Simulation Center.

System 02 / Under pressure

Where autonomy actually fails

Every stage of the AutoDrive stack worked on the bench. The failures lived in the arrows between them.

Perception

sensor uncertainty. ~85% accuracy on the test set is not 100%.

Localization

timing. Every estimate is already stale by the time it is used.

Decision

integration failures. Correct nodes, wrong assumptions between them.

CAN communication

message formatting. One malformed frame reads as a command.

Vehicle response
Incident log / entry 001 Garage test, protocol changes
Incident
Test command sent with the team lead in the driver's seat and an emergency stop armed. The car lurched forward, accelerated, and plowed straight into a workbench.
Cause
A malformed CAN message that the vehicle ECU interpreted as "go."
Response
Nobody was hurt. The car was fine, mostly. The following week went to tracing exactly what happened.
Result
1 table destroyed. Safety isn't optional.

Method

Operating principles

01

Integrate before you optimize

The hardest failures often exist between components, not inside them.

Seen in: Terrabot's five-subsystem integration AutoDrive CAN timing CAST, RFID + audio + UI made invisible

02

Design for the person using it

A technically correct system can still fail if its workflow is confusing.

Seen in: ACA's read-type-one-number interface A filename protocol handed to non-programmers CAST, built for nursing students, not engineers

03

Build for the second use

A prototype proves the idea. Documentation, reliability, and maintainability make it useful.

Seen in: World Cup Fantasy → La Liga fork, ~80% reuse A Commerce-only dashboard made config-driven Ops scripts that default to dry-run
