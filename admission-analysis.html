Universal Admission Dashboard

Ten years of admission records, a Random Forest predictor ranking, a policy what-if simulator, and an anonymized student lookup, behind one program-aware desktop dashboard. Written up here the way I would defend it in a meeting: what the data shows, what it does not, and where the analysis stops and the institution's judgment begins.

Decision context

Queen's sets admission thresholds for each program: minimum grades in specific subjects and a minimum overall high school average. Those numbers decide who gets an offer. When I joined Undergraduate Admission and Recruitment in May 2025, threshold discussions ran mostly on experience and spreadsheet summaries. The university collects detailed admission data on every student, then tracks their first-year GPA after they enroll, but connecting the two takes analysis time nobody had.

The stakes of getting this right are real. Set a threshold too low and students are admitted into programs where they struggle. Set it too high, or weight the wrong input, and capable students are turned away. Any analysis feeding that decision should be explicit about its own limits, which is what this page tries to be.

Dataset

Two datasets, one per program, each covering roughly 10 years of admitted students. Commerce: about 5,600 students. Engineering: about 8,700 students, spread across six common-first-year disciplines. Each record pairs admission inputs, overall high school average, individual subject grades, supplementary application score, admission curriculum (Ontario, IB, and so on), with the outcome variable, first-year GPA. The raw material arrived as difficult Excel exports, cleaned with pandas and openpyxl and loaded into SQLite for querying.

Privacy was handled before analysis, not after. anonymize_commerce_data.py hashes real student numbers into anonymous IDs with SHA-256, and the only file linking the two is misc/student_id_mapping.json. Analysts can explore grades and GPA freely without a real ID ever appearing on screen, while that one mapping file is the sensitive key that gets locked down separately. The anonymized lookup table, built from per-term sheets in anonymized_commerce_data.xlsx, holds about 5,087 students.

One property of this dataset shapes everything downstream: it contains only students who were admitted and enrolled. That matters later, in Limitations.

The question

Which admission inputs predict first-year academic performance, and how strongly? Stated carefully, that is a question about prediction, not about cause. Two follow-up questions came with it: if a threshold had been set differently, how would the historical population have split, and would a proposed threshold fall harder on students from one admission curriculum than another?

Methodology

The core analysis is a Random Forest model (scikit-learn) regressing first-year GPA on the admission inputs, used to rank predictors by feature importance. I chose a Random Forest because it handles nonlinear relationships and mixed inputs without assuming a functional form. Its importance ranking is relative to the model and the other variables it was given, a point that matters in Interpretation. Alongside the model, the pipeline runs plain correlations checked across years, clustering, retention analysis, and bootstrap confidence intervals, producing about a dozen PNG figures per run.

The instrument

The analysis started as a Commerce-only script during the Recruitment Representative role, because the by-hand version, correlating each input against GPA and re-checking it across years, took long enough that building a tool won out over repeating the manual process every time someone asked a new question. During the Business Analyst role I turned it into one program-aware system: universal_dashboard.py, a single UniversalDashboard class launched by run_dashboard.py, with a program-selector dropdown and four tabs, Main Dashboard, Course Analysis, Policy Threshold, and Student Lookup.

Choosing a program swaps which analysis engine runs underneath. Commerce stays on its original hardcoded legacy class, because it was built before the system was designed to be generic at all. Every other program reads its own programs/<name>/config.yaml, column mappings, predictors, thresholds, through a generic GenericAdmissionAnalysis class. That is the universal part: adding a new program is mostly a config file, not new code.

Disclosure / Unwired features

An Extended Analysis tab and a Feature Importance panel are fully built in the code but not reachable in the running app right now. One tab is never attached to the notebook, the other's display call is commented out. Working code that is not currently wired in is not a shipped feature, so it is not counted as one here.

Policy Threshold simulator

policy_threshold_dashboard.py is a rule-based what-if tool, not an optimizer. It does not calculate the best cutoff. You type in thresholds, for example Math ≥ 88 and English ≥ 82, and it partitions the historical student population by that AND-rule and shows, live: how many students would pass or fail, the GPA gap between the two groups with a t-test for whether that gap is statistically distinguishable from noise, and a rejection-rate breakdown by admission curriculum, Ontario versus IB and so on, flagging whether a proposed policy would disproportionately hit one group. That fairness breakdown is the part that is easy to miss when eyeballing spreadsheets, and it turns a gut call into an exportable, checkable justification.

Student Lookup

student_lookup.py loads every sheet of the anonymized workbook, one sheet per admission term, into a single in-memory table and supports search by anonymous ID, real ID, or criteria: GPA range, program, admit term, school type, grade, subject, course. It replaces manually scrolling through per-term Excel tabs to pull one student's file for a committee discussion.

Assumptions

Every result below rests on these. If one of them is wrong, the conclusions weaken accordingly.

  • First-year GPA is the measure of success. It is a proxy. It does not capture everything a program values in a student.
  • The dataset covers admitted students who enrolled. We never observe how rejected applicants would have performed, so every relationship is measured inside an already-filtered population.
  • Grades are treated as comparable across 10 years and across admission curricula. Grade inflation and different grading cultures make this only approximately true, which is part of why the per-curriculum breakdown exists.
  • Past cohorts are assumed to resemble future ones closely enough for historical patterns to inform forward-looking policy.
  • Supplementary application scores, as graded, are taken as a faithful measure of what the supplementary application is meant to assess.

Analysis

Commerce ran first. The Random Forest was trained on the roughly 5,600-student dataset, importance rankings were pulled from the fitted model, and each ranked predictor was cross-checked against its raw correlation with first-year GPA, per year and pooled. The Course Analysis tab breaks the same question down further, examining predictor strength at the level of individual first-year courses rather than overall GPA.

Once the generic engine existed, the same pipeline was pointed at Engineering: roughly 8,700 admitted students over 10 years. The bigger dataset and different program structure, six common-first-year disciplines instead of one Commerce track, required rethinking the predictor variables and how to handle program transfers. That model is built and running. Its findings are still being reviewed internally and no policy discussion has come from it yet, so this writeup deliberately reports only the Commerce results.

Results

For Commerce, the predictor ranking came out as follows.

Best predictor: overall high school average (no surprise).
Second: Math grade.
Third: English grade.
Fourth: supplementary application score, and its raw correlation with first-year GPA was slightly negative.

The first three lined up with intuition. The fourth did not. The supplementary application, essays, extracurriculars, the "soft" inputs, ranked last in predictive importance, and separately, its plain correlation with first-year GPA ran slightly negative. Students who scored well on it were not performing better in first year, and in this dataset, marginally worse.

It is tempting to jump to explanations. Maybe strong applications reflect skill at self-presentation rather than at coursework. Maybe the grading rubric rewarded the wrong things. Both are speculation, and I flagged them as such at the time. What the data supports saying is narrower: within this admitted population, over these 10 years, the supplementary application score did not help predict first-year GPA, and its association with GPA was slightly negative. That was worth putting in front of people, and it was the finding that got the most attention.

Limitations

This is the section I would want read most carefully.

  • Correlation is not causation. Nothing here shows that any admission input causes first-year performance. There is no experiment and no counterfactual: we never observe how students below a threshold would have done, because they were never admitted.
  • Selection effects can manufacture correlations. The dataset is conditioned on admission. If applicants with borderline grades tended to need a strong supplementary application to get in, selection alone could produce a negative association between supp-app score and GPA inside the admitted group, even if no such relationship exists among applicants at large. I cannot rule this out with this data.
  • Feature importance is model-relative. A Random Forest's ranking says how much each variable helped this model reduce error given the other variables present. Correlated predictors can take importance from each other. It is not a measure of real-world effect size.
  • GPA is a narrow outcome. First-year GPA does not measure leadership, persistence through personal difficulty, or contribution to a program's community, which are things a supplementary application may exist to find.
  • Attribution is unknowable from where I sat. I am not going to claim my analysis changed the thresholds. I was not in the rooms where the decision was made, there is no documented decision trail assigning weight to this work, and universities do not change policy on one analyst's chart.

Interpretation

Four different things get collapsed into each other in casual conversation about analysis like this. Keeping them separate is most of the discipline.

Correlation

An observed association in this dataset. The supplementary application's slight negative correlation with first-year GPA is exactly this, and only this.

Predictive importance

How much a variable helps a specific model reduce error, relative to the other variables it was given. The Random Forest ranking is this. It is a useful ordering, not a strength-of-effect measurement.

Causation

Whether changing an input would change the outcome. Nothing in this analysis establishes it. Getting there would require an experiment or an identification strategy that admissions data does not offer.

Organizational decision-making

How a university actually changes policy. Committees weigh evidence alongside precedent, enrollment targets, fairness, and strategy. An analysis is one input into that process, never the process itself.

Reading the results through that ledger: the predictor ranking held up across years and is worth taking seriously as prediction. The negative supp-app correlation is worth investigating, and not worth acting on alone, because at least one mechanical explanation (selection) could produce it without any real underlying relationship.

Learning to hold those distinctions was the hardest part of the project, harder than the code. I spent a lot of time second-guessing myself: is this correlation real or did I mess something up, does this p-value actually matter, am I seeing a pattern or noise. Learning to look from different angles, controlling for variables, checking for confounders, was the real education.

Organizational context

The analysis was shared and became part of the threshold conversation for Commerce. The program later raised its admission thresholds:

  • Math and English minimums: 80% to 85%
  • Overall high school average: 87% to 90%

I'm not going to claim I walked into a room, said "change your thresholds," and they did it. That's not how universities work. The analysis was part of the conversation, the change aligned with what the numbers suggested, and that is as far as the evidence goes.

The organizational outcome I can attribute to this work is the tooling itself. What began as a one-program script is now a system the office keeps using: the policy simulator replaces re-filtering spreadsheets every time someone proposes a cutoff, the lookup replaces scrolling per-term Excel tabs before committee meetings, and a new program plugs in through a config file rather than new code. The Automatic Recruiter Coordinator, the office's feeder-school and regional analysis tool, remains a deliberate exception: a standalone companion with its own CLI and GUI, not wired into this umbrella dashboard.

The Engineering analysis is the open thread. The model runs, the findings are under review, and whether they enter a policy conversation is, correctly, not my call to make.
